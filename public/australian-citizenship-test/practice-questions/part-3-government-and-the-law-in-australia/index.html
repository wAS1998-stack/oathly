Part 3: Government and the law in Australia

70 practice questions with answers and explanations for Part 3 of the Australian citizenship test. The correct answer is marked under each question.

Take a practice test

  1. 1. What must Australian citizens aged 18 or over do before they can vote?

    • Join a political party
    • Enrol to vote (correct)
    • Pass a test on the Constitution

    Citizens aged 18 or over must enrol to vote, and people who are not correctly enrolled cannot vote in an election.

  2. 2. Which organisation conducts federal elections and referendums?

    • The Australian Electoral Commission (AEC) (correct)
    • The Australian Taxation Office (ATO)
    • Services Australia

    The AEC runs federal elections and referendums and maintains the Commonwealth electoral roll.

  3. 3. Which statement about the Australian Electoral Commission is correct?

    • It is run by the largest political party
    • It is independent of the government (correct)
    • It is controlled by the Prime Minister

    The AEC is independent, and political parties or people in government cannot influence its decisions.

  4. 4. What does it mean that voting in Australia is by secret ballot?

    • Your vote is read out at the polling place
    • Your employer is told how you voted
    • You vote in private and are free to choose any candidate (correct)

    Because voting is by secret ballot, you are free and safe to vote for any candidate you choose.

  5. 5. What may happen if you fail to vote in an election without a good reason?

    • You may have to pay a fine (correct)
    • You will lose your Australian citizenship
    • You will be banned from voting in future elections

    Voting is compulsory, so failing to vote without a good reason may result in a fine.

  6. 6. How can citizens raise concerns about government policy?

    • By contacting their elected representative (correct)
    • By asking a judge to change the policy
    • By asking the police to change the policy

    Citizens can contact their elected representative about government policy, and the representative should consider their suggestions.

  7. 7. Before 1901, how was Australia organised?

    • As six separate, self-governing British colonies (correct)
    • As one British colony run from Sydney
    • As eight independent countries

    After British settlement and before 1901, Australia was made up of six separate British colonies, each with its own constitution and laws.

  8. 8. Which problem did the separate colonies have before federation?

    • Trade and transport between them was expensive and slow (correct)
    • They had no laws or constitutions of their own
    • They were all governed by one shared parliament

    Before federation, trade and transport between colonies was slow and costly, enforcing laws across borders was hard, and defence was weak.

  9. 9. On what date did the Australian Constitution come into effect?

    • 26 January 1788
    • 25 April 1915
    • 1 January 1901 (correct)

    The Constitution came into effect on 1 January 1901, when the colonies became one nation, the Commonwealth of Australia.

  10. 10. What is the Australian Constitution?

    • A list of Australia's national symbols
    • The pledge new citizens make at their ceremony
    • The legal document setting out the basic rules of government (correct)

    The Constitution is the legal document that sets out the basic rules for how Australia is governed.

  11. 11. How was the Australian Constitution originally passed?

    • By a vote of all Australians in the 1967 Referendum
    • As part of a British Act of Parliament in 1900 (correct)
    • By a decision of the High Court of Australia in 1901

    The Constitution was first passed as part of a British Act of Parliament in 1900, and it came into effect in 1901.

  12. 12. Which two Houses make up the Parliament of the Commonwealth of Australia?

    • The House of Representatives and the Cabinet
    • The House of Representatives and the Senate (correct)
    • The Senate and the High Court

    The Constitution set up the Australian Parliament with two Houses: the House of Representatives and the Senate.

  13. 13. Which body has the ultimate power to apply and interpret the laws of Australia?

    • The Senate
    • The High Court of Australia (correct)
    • The Australian Electoral Commission

    The Constitution also set up the High Court, which has the final say in applying and interpreting Australia's laws.

  14. 14. Who can change the Australian Constitution?

    • The Australian people, by voting in a referendum (correct)
    • The Prime Minister and Cabinet, by a Cabinet vote
    • The Governor-General, by signing an order

    The Constitution can be changed when the Australian people vote for the change in a referendum.

  15. 15. In the 1967 Referendum, what share of Australians voted 'Yes'?

    • About 50 per cent
    • More than 90 per cent (correct)
    • Less than 25 per cent

    More than 90 per cent of Australians voted 'Yes' in the 1967 Referendum.

  16. 16. What change did the 1967 Referendum allow?

    • The colonies to join together as one nation
    • Voting to become compulsory
    • Aboriginal peoples to be counted in the Census (correct)

    The 1967 Referendum changed the Constitution so that Aboriginal peoples could be counted in the Census.

  17. 17. What is needed for a referendum to change the Australian Constitution?

    • The approval of the Governor-General only
    • A majority of voters in any one state
    • A 'double majority' (correct)

    A double majority means a majority of voters across the nation and a majority of voters in a majority of states must vote for the change.

  18. 18. Why does the Constitution divide the power of government into three branches?

    • To allow laws to be made more quickly
    • To give the King a day-to-day role in government
    • To stop one person or group from holding all the power (correct)

    Power is split between the legislative, executive and judicial branches so that no single person or group holds all of it.

  19. 19. What is legislative power?

    • The power to make and change laws (correct)
    • The power to put laws into practice
    • The power to interpret and apply laws

    Legislative power belongs to Parliament, whose members are elected by the people.

  20. 20. What is the power to put laws into practice called?

    • Legislative power
    • Executive power (correct)
    • Judicial power

    Executive power puts laws into practice; the Executive includes the Prime Minister, government ministers and the Governor-General.

  21. 21. Who has the power to interpret and apply the law?

    • Government ministers
    • Members of Parliament
    • Judges (correct)

    Judicial power belongs to judges, and courts and judges are independent of parliament and government.

  22. 22. Who is Australia's Head of State?

    • The King of Australia (correct)
    • The Prime Minister
    • The Governor-General

    The King of Australia is the Head of State, and the Governor-General is his representative in Australia.

  23. 23. Who appoints the Governor-General?

    • The King, on the advice of the Prime Minister (correct)
    • The voters, at a federal election
    • The Senate, by a vote of its members

    The King of Australia appoints the Governor-General as his representative, acting on advice from the Prime Minister.

  24. 24. What does it mean that Australia is a constitutional monarchy?

    • The King runs the government day to day
    • The Prime Minister is also the Head of State
    • The King is Head of State but must act in line with the Constitution (correct)

    In a constitutional monarchy the King is Head of State, but he must act in accordance with the Constitution.

  25. 25. Which statement about the Governor-General is correct?

    • The Governor-General leads the largest political party
    • The Governor-General must remain neutral (correct)
    • The Governor-General is elected by the Senate

    The Governor-General is not part of the government, acts independently of all political parties and must stay neutral.

  26. 26. What is it called when the Governor-General signs a Bill passed by Parliament into law?

    • A referendum
    • A double majority
    • Royal Assent (correct)

    Signing Bills passed by Parliament into law is called Royal Assent and is one of the Governor-General's roles.

  27. 27. Who is the Commander-in-Chief of the Australian Defence Force?

    • The Governor-General (correct)
    • The Prime Minister
    • The Premier of each state

    Acting as Commander-in-Chief of the Australian Defence Force is one of the Governor-General's roles.

  28. 28. Which of these is a role of the Governor-General?

    • Starting the process for a federal election (correct)
    • Leading the Opposition in Parliament
    • Running local councils

    The Governor-General's roles include starting the process for a federal election, performing ceremonial duties and giving Royal Assent.

  29. 29. What is the title of the leader of a state government?

    • Chief Minister
    • Premier (correct)
    • Governor

    A state government is led by the Premier, while a territory government is led by the Chief Minister.

  30. 30. What is the title of the leader of a territory government?

    • Premier
    • Chief Minister (correct)
    • Administrator

    The leader of a territory government is the Chief Minister.

  31. 31. Who represents the King in each Australian state?

    • The Premier
    • The Mayor
    • The Governor (correct)

    Each state has a Governor who represents the King, in a role similar to the Governor-General's.

  32. 32. Who is the leader of a local government?

    • A Premier or Chief Minister
    • A Governor or Administrator
    • A Mayor or Shire President (correct)

    A local government is led by a Mayor or Shire President, and councillors are the elected members of the council.

  33. 33. What is the Australian Government also called?

    • The State Government or the Local Council
    • The Federal Government or the Commonwealth Government (correct)
    • The Crown Government or the Royal Government

    The Australian Government is also known as the Federal Government or the Commonwealth Government.

  34. 34. What is another name for the House of Representatives?

    • The States' House
    • The People's House (correct)
    • The House of Review

    The House of Representatives is also known as the Lower House or the People's House; the States' House and the House of Review are names for the Senate.

  35. 35. Who does each member of the House of Representatives represent?

    • Only the members of their political party
    • A whole state, with every state equally represented
    • The people in their federal electorate (correct)

    Australia is divided into federal electorates, and each member of the House of Representatives represents the people in their electorate.

  36. 36. How is the number of House of Representatives members for each state and territory decided?

    • By the number of people living there (correct)
    • Every state and territory has the same number
    • By the land area of the state or territory

    The number of MPs for each state and territory is based on how many people live there.

  37. 37. Which House of Parliament is also called the States' House?

    • The Senate (correct)
    • The House of Representatives
    • The Cabinet

    The Senate is sometimes called the Upper House, the House of Review or the States' House.

  38. 38. How many senators are there in total?

    • 12
    • Over 150
    • 76 (correct)

    There are 76 senators in total: 12 from each state and two from each of the two mainland territories.

  39. 39. How many senators does each state elect?

    • 6
    • 76
    • 12 (correct)

    Each state elects 12 senators, so all states are equally represented regardless of size or population.

  40. 40. How many senators do the Australian Capital Territory and the Northern Territory each elect?

    • Twelve
    • Six
    • Two (correct)

    The Australian Capital Territory and the Northern Territory elect two senators each.

  41. 41. Who appoints the Administrator of the Northern Territory?

    • The Governor-General (correct)
    • The Chief Minister
    • The voters of the Northern Territory

    The Administrator of the Northern Territory is appointed by the Governor-General and has a role similar to that of a state Governor.

  42. 42. How do the states differ from the territories?

    • States have rights recognised by the Constitution; territories do not (correct)
    • Territories have their own constitutions; states do not
    • Only territories can pass their own laws

    States have constitutional rights and can pass laws in their own right, while the Australian Government can change or revoke self-governing territory laws.

  43. 43. What do the Australian states each have?

    • Their own national defence force
    • Their own parliament and constitution (correct)
    • Their own separate Head of State

    Each state government has its own parliament and constitution, and works in a similar way to the Australian Government.

  44. 44. Which level of government is responsible for rubbish collection?

    • State governments
    • Local government (correct)
    • The Australian Government

    Local governments are responsible for services such as rubbish collection, local roads, parks and libraries.

  45. 45. Which level of government is mainly responsible for hospitals and schools?

    • Local governments
    • The Australian Government
    • State and territory governments (correct)

    State and territory governments are primarily responsible for hospitals and health services, schools, police and public transport.

  46. 46. Which level of government is responsible for immigration and citizenship?

    • State governments
    • Local governments
    • The Australian Government (correct)

    The Australian Government handles immigration and citizenship, along with defence, foreign affairs, taxation and social security.

  47. 47. Which of these is a responsibility of the Australian Government?

    • Rubbish collection
    • Public transport
    • Defence (correct)

    Defence is an Australian Government responsibility, while rubbish collection is handled by local government and public transport by state and territory governments.

  48. 48. Which level of government is responsible for building permits?

    • The Australian Government
    • Local government (correct)
    • State governments

    Local governments look after building permits, as well as parks, drains and local libraries.

  49. 49. What is a political party?

    • A group of people who share ideas about how a country should be governed (correct)
    • A court of law that hears cases about disputed elections
    • A government department that collects tax from voters

    A political party is a group of people with similar ideas about how the country should be governed; the main parties include the Liberal Party, Labor, the Nationals and the Greens.

  50. 50. What are members of parliament without a political party called?

    • Independents (correct)
    • Senators
    • Ministers

    Most members of parliament belong to a political party; those who do not are called independents.

  51. 51. After a federal election, who generally forms the Australian Government?

    • The party with the most members in the Senate
    • The party or coalition with a majority in the House of Representatives (correct)
    • The party with the most members in state parliaments

    The government is generally formed by the party or coalition with a majority in the House of Representatives, and its leader becomes Prime Minister.

  52. 52. What is the party or coalition with the second largest number of members in the House of Representatives called?

    • The Cabinet
    • The Opposition (correct)
    • The Senate

    The party or coalition with the second largest number of members forms the Opposition, led by the Leader of the Opposition.

  53. 53. What is the Cabinet?

    • The key decision-making body of government (correct)
    • The lower house of a state parliament
    • The group that conducts federal elections

    The Cabinet is made up of the ministers with the most important portfolios and is the government's key decision-making body.

  54. 54. Who approves the appointment of the Prime Minister and ministers?

    • The Governor-General (correct)
    • The Senate
    • The Leader of the Opposition

    The Prime Minister recommends who should be ministers, and the Governor-General approves the appointment of the Prime Minister and ministers.

  55. 55. What is a proposal for a new law, or a change to an existing law, called?

    • A referendum
    • A portfolio
    • A Bill (correct)

    A proposed new law or change to a law is called a Bill, and both Houses of Parliament debate and vote on it.

  56. 56. What happens to a Bill after a majority in each House of Parliament agrees to it?

    • It goes to a referendum of all voters
    • It goes to the High Court for approval
    • It goes to the Governor-General to be signed into law (correct)

    Once both Houses pass a Bill, it goes to the Governor-General, who signs it into law.

  57. 57. What does a court decide in a criminal case?

    • Which political party forms the government
    • Whether a person has broken the law and, if so, the penalty (correct)
    • Who should be elected to the Senate

    Australian courts are independent and decide whether a person has broken the law and what the penalty is, based only on the evidence.

  58. 58. What can courts base their decisions on?

    • What the police tell them to decide
    • What the government wants
    • Only the evidence before them (correct)

    Courts can only base their decisions on the evidence put before them.

  59. 59. Can the government remove a judge because it disagrees with the judge's decisions?

    • No, judges are independent (correct)
    • Yes, the Prime Minister can remove any judge
    • Yes, if most voters agree

    Judges and magistrates are appointed by the government, but the government cannot take away their jobs because it disagrees with their decisions.

  60. 60. In Australia, how is a person treated until they are found guilty in a court?

    • As guilty
    • As innocent (correct)
    • As a suspect who has no rights

    In Australia, people are considered innocent until a court finds them guilty, and everyone can be represented by a lawyer.

  61. 61. In a criminal trial, who decides the penalty if the jury finds a person guilty?

    • The judge (correct)
    • The jury
    • The police

    The judge explains the law to the jury, and if the jury finds a person guilty, the judge decides the penalty.

  62. 62. How do the police relate to the government in Australia?

    • The police take their orders from the Prime Minister
    • The police are run by the largest political party
    • The police are independent of the government (correct)

    Police maintain peace and order and are independent of the government, but it is the court that decides guilt.

  63. 63. What is the name of Australia's national police force?

    • The Australian Defence Force (ADF)
    • The Australian Federal Police (AFP) (correct)
    • The Australian Electoral Commission (AEC)

    The Australian Federal Police is the national police force; it investigates crimes against federal laws.

  64. 64. What does the Australian Federal Police investigate?

    • Crimes against federal laws, such as drug trafficking (correct)
    • Breaches of local council building permits
    • Tax returns that are lodged late

    The AFP investigates crimes against federal laws, such as drug trafficking and crimes against national security or the environment.

  65. 65. Is it legal to offer a bribe to a police officer in Australia?

    • Yes, if the amount of money is small
    • No, even offering a bribe is a crime (correct)
    • Yes, for minor offences such as parking

    Bribing police is a serious crime in Australia, and even offering a bribe to a police officer is a crime.

  66. 66. A person breaks a law they were unaware of. Can they use this as an excuse?

    • No, not knowing the law is no excuse (correct)
    • Yes, if they are new to Australia
    • Yes, if it is their first offence

    Everyone must become familiar with Australian laws, because not knowing the law is no excuse for breaking it.

  67. 67. Which statement about domestic and family violence is correct?

    • It is a private family matter and not a crime
    • It is allowed if it happens inside the home
    • It is against the law (correct)

    Domestic and family violence, such as hitting or controlling a partner through fear, is against the law, and a person who commits it can go to jail.

  68. 68. Who controls road and traffic rules in Australia?

    • The Australian Government
    • State and territory governments (correct)
    • The High Court of Australia

    Road and traffic rules are controlled by state and territory governments, and breaking them can lead to a fine or prison.

  69. 69. Who must wear a seatbelt when travelling in a car in Australia?

    • Only the driver
    • Everybody in the car (correct)
    • Only passengers in the front seat

    Everybody travelling in a car must wear a seatbelt, and babies and young children must be in an approved car seat.

  70. 70. Which of these is against the law while driving in Australia?

    • Using a hand-held mobile device (correct)
    • Listening to the radio
    • Driving with the windows open

    Traffic laws are strict: it is illegal to use a hand-held mobile device while driving, or to drive after taking drugs or above the blood alcohol limit.
